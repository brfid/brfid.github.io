## Introduction

I generated this with Astra in response to questions posed by John Day. The account follows the effort to separate a destination's identity from its network attachments, from early naming proposals through implementations and the standards of the 1980s. It traces which mechanisms entered use, which remained proposals, and how Internet specifications divided responsibility for multihoming.

Host multihoming concerns one communicating destination reachable through several network attachments. The questions are which entity a design names, who selects an attachment, and whether an existing conversation can continue after an attachment fails. A router forwarding a packet is a relay on its path; it acts as a destination when the communication terminates there.

The historical distinction is between providing several ways to reach a destination and making their use a common responsibility of the network. CYCLADES and BBN developed mechanisms for preserving destination identity, while the 1989 Internet requirements left remote multihoming to applications. The sources document both practical adoption and continuing disagreement over the scope of a common service. Proposals, implementation reports, and demonstrated behavior need to remain distinct throughout that history.

## Walden separated communication endpoints from sites

In 1970, David Walden was helping build the ARPANET at BBN. His [RFC 61](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc61.html) (July), revised as [RFC 62](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc62.html) (August), drew on that work to consider how communication could work in an ideal network. He proposed separating communication endpoints from the sites hosting them: port numbers named transferable endpoints, while separate site numbers located them. An endpoint could retain its identity while passing to another process on another host.[^walden]

Walden proposed placing the rendezvous machinery in the IMPs. He explicitly credited Ackerman and Plummer's MIT PDP-1 work, *An Implementation of a Multiprocessing Computer System* (1967). His proposal was unimplemented and did not represent an official BBN position.

Walden addressed a moving endpoint; host multihoming concerns a continuing host with several attachments. The shared problem was how to identify a destination independently of where packets reached it. Direct descent of the later BBN designs from his proposal remains unestablished.

## CYCLADES put the recipient beyond the attachment

In January 1973, [Louis Pouzin's addressing note for CYCLADES](https://brfid.github.io/posts/multihoming/scans/pouzin-inwg20-p2.png) argued that a recipient reachable through several network exit points could not be identified by one exit position. He also questioned treating a physical computer as the communicating identity: coupled computers and virtual machines made that correspondence unreliable. His preferred object of identification was the communications software.[^cyclades]

The distinction had a routing mechanism behind it. The proposed transport-station names identified destinations; forwarding tables associated a destination with an output line or a weight used in choosing one. Regional aggregation was intended to reduce the table size, while local station entries supplied more specific destinations. The destination name did not have to become the number of the selected exit. The following illustrates the intended property, rather than a recovered network configuration or failover test:

```
Packet destination remains station E

             +-- node A -- attachment a --+
CIGALE ------+                           +--> E
             +-- node B -- attachment b --+
```

Routing could select another way to reach E without requiring the sender to substitute a different destination name. This did not make attachment knowledge or route maintenance disappear: those remained work for the communications subnet. CYCLADES separated naming the recipient from choosing how to reach it.[^cyclades]

That year, [Cerf's ITP draft](https://brfid.github.io/posts/multihoming/scans/cerf-itp-1973-pdf27.png) explicitly allowed a host to attach to several packet switches in the same or different networks. The definition recognized multiple attachments without specifying how to select among them or preserve a connection after failure.[^itp]

These ideas circulated among the designers of internetworking. Cerf and Kahn's [1974 paper](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall06/cos561/papers/cerf74.pdf#page=13) cited Pouzin's addressing note; INWG 48 recorded joint packet-format work by Pouzin, Cerf, and Sunshine. BBN's own 1974 report explicitly debated CYCLADES over the division of work between hosts and the subnet, in the same treatment that discussed multiple host attachments.[^exchange] The record establishes contact and engagement. It does not establish that each later BBN or MIT mechanism descended from Pouzin's particular proposal.

Pouzin's internetwork proposals also separated naming from the choice of an entrance. His October 1973 and March 1974 designs named packet-switching networks while allowing gateway selection to change. They permitted naming areas to overlap physical networks under compatible naming conventions.[^cyclades] A network field could therefore coexist with a continuing destination identity. Extending that identity across independently administered networks still required an agreed naming and delivery arrangement.

The April 1974 CYCLADES transport specification defined the machinery above packet delivery. A transport station, associated with a virtual installation, had its own identifying number. Packet context included source and destination station numbers; a flow joined ports within that context. Error control retransmitted unacknowledged letters, discarded duplicates, and terminated the session after repeated unsuccessful transmissions.[^cyclades-transport] The resulting recovery argument is conditional: a conversation could continue through another attachment if routing recovered while the station's transport state remained valid and before its retry limit was exhausted. That is a deduction from the design, not a recorded failure experiment.

Pouzin's [1974 account of CIGALE](https://rogerdmoore.info/PS/CIGALE/CIGALE.html) supplied a stronger implementation claim. It described the independent address space as implemented, allowed delivery to the same host address from different nodes, and permitted traffic over multiple host-to-node lines. Its routing discussion described propagated delay and buffer information per destination region or local host.[^cigale] CYCLADES therefore has contemporary evidence for implemented destination naming independent of attachment, beyond a proposal to connect a computer twice. The same account restricted hosts spanning different routing regions because their additional entries could make tables too large. Preserving identity was compatible with aggregation, but did not remove the cost of representing exceptional attachments.

The remaining implementation questions are more specific. The material examined does not establish the exact procedures for registering and withdrawing a station's attachments, or identify a dated test in which an existing flow survived the loss of one of two attachment nodes. Pouzin's 1975 status report documents an operating network, with a first public demonstration in November 1973 and expansion to several switches in 1974. The first demonstration used one packet switch; it cannot establish recovery through a second switch.[^cyclades-operation]

An April 1977 Waterloo progress report documents failure-recovery simulation. Its CIGALE-based simulator had been used to study route recovery after link or switch failure, citing Irland and Macdonald's May 1976 report E-49. The investigators checked the simulator against program traces, calculated cases and other simulations, but said adequate real-network measurements were unavailable for validating their predictions. This supplies a dated simulation trail. It does not identify an existing transport conversation surviving a failed attachment or establish the exact attachment-management procedures running in CIGALE.[^cigale-simulation]

Contemporary reports also document measurements on working CIGALE software. In April 1977, Gelenbe, Grangé and Mussard described two nodes running the regular CIGALE system, its TMM line protocol and an artificial traffic generator over a 19.2 kbit/s laboratory line. In June 1978, Eyries and Pujolle described experiments using MESCIG to generate packets and OBSERVER to record their timing. Their topology connected an IRIS 80 host to one switch, M3, with several paths among the switches. The experiments deliberately specified routes in packet text.[^cigale-measurements]

These measurements establish functioning forwarding and line-control software. The reported retry timers govern retransmission on a line; they do not establish the time to declare an attachment dead, withdraw its route, or preserve an existing conversation through another attachment.[^cigale-measurements] CYCLADES supplies a substantive architectural comparison, with a remaining gap between the documented mechanisms and a dated conversation-preserving failure test.

## BBN and PARC developed ways to use several attachments

BBN's January 1974 PWIN report recommended experiments with hosts attached to more than one IMP. Its [October analysis](https://brfid.github.io/posts/multihoming/scans/bbn-2913-p57.png) treated mapping a logical host name to several physical IMP ports as straightforward in principle; coordinating sequencing and error-control state across IMPs was harder.[^bbn-design]

BBN authors also explicitly recognized the CYCLADES advantage. In their January 1975 course notes, John McQuillan and David Walden explained that addressing hosts instead of switching nodes, together with leaving packet ordering out of the subnet, allowed a host to connect simultaneously to multiple nodes.[^cyclades-bbn]

At PARC, Ed Taft's [January 1975 Pup memo](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2024/10/102644058-05-001-acc.pdf#page=57) associated a host or process name with several addresses. Once chosen, connection addresses remained fixed. Yet a packet addressed to Maxc's Ethernet attachment could arrive through its MCA attachment and be forwarded internally. Taft explicitly described preserving communication after network failure. This was a concrete design for separating a connection's address from its physical delivery path; the cited memo does not date its first operational demonstration.[^pup]

At BBN, Falk and McQuillan's September 1975 AUTODIN II study compared several ways to distribute traffic and recover after an access-circuit failure.[^bbn-design] The [1977 ARPANET proposal](https://brfid.github.io/posts/multihoming/scans/p77-csy-93a-p10.jpg), submitted to Cerf, separated logical identities from physical connections and proposed rerouting channels after port failure. Its retained text explicitly said ARPANET did not yet support dual homing of hosts.[^bbn]

The naming discussion continued across PARC and ISI. In January 1978, Shoch's [IEN 19](https://www.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien19.txt) described multiple addresses and acknowledged BBN routing work; Cohen's [IEN 23](https://www.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien23.pdf) treated processes as communication endpoints and examined the bindings among names, addresses, and routes.[^naming-debate]

## BBN put host identity into routing

BBN's 1979 report described TENEX hosts already running NCP, IP, and TCP on both ARPANET and RCCNET, with interface choices built into the monitor.[^host-implementations] The same report proposed a more general routing mechanism across networks.[^bbn]

The proposed lookup order was **full destination host identity first, network group second**. A host entry could direct traffic through an attachment on B even when the address's apparent location lay on A. When no host entry existed, forwarding could use the network-group route. The [1979 failure example](https://brfid.github.io/posts/multihoming/scans/p79-isd-76-pp74-75.jpg) explicitly retained the destination name while delivering through the other interface.

This made the interpretation of the address depend on routing state. The initial proposal retained a 32-bit destination and limited lookup to an exact host match followed by the network fallback. It also considered suppressing redundant routing information.[^bbn] The required work was to maintain reachability for the continuing host identity and select another attachment after failure. The source still called for [experiments and trial implementations](https://brfid.github.io/posts/multihoming/scans/p79-isd-76-pp76-77.jpg); operating two interfaces did not establish deployment of this routing scheme.

## Cerf proposed recovery above TCP and IP

In [August 1979, Cerf examined the recovery problem in TCP/IP](https://www.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien110.txt). Citing a private note from Plummer and Tomlinson, he described how a host with two network attachments could lose a TCP connection when one interface failed: the connection used an address tied to that interface. Reconnecting through the other address would create a new TCP connection.[^tcp]

```
            Client
       (connected to a)
              |
     +--------+--------+
     |                 |
 Network A         Network B
     |                 |
     X failed          | working
     |                 |
     a                 b
 +---+-----------------+---+
 |         Host H          |
 |      still running      |
 +-------------------------+
```

Cerf proposed placing recovery above TCP/IP, using name servers to supply multiple addresses. He considered keeping information about every host in every gateway prohibitively expensive. That objection applied to a particular distribution of state; stable host identity did not inherently require that arrangement. He presented his recommendations as a strawman for discussion.[^debate]

## ISI and MIT developed alternatives in 1980

In March 1980, [Sunshine and Postel proposed fixed virtual addresses](https://www.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien135.pdf) whose location mappings could change, explicitly discussing mobility, multihoming, and TCP's dependence on an address.[^naming-debate]

[Saltzer's March 1980 campus proposal](https://www.mit.edu/~Saltzer/publications/lnn/csr-lnn-022.pdf) separated identifying the target from obtaining a route. It belonged to MIT's local-network program and drew on source-routing and naming work from several groups.[^mit-antecedents] A routing service could choose a reachable attachment and return a source route:

**target H → routing service → sequence of local-network addresses → selected attachment**

A different route could be obtained without requiring the originator to enumerate H's attachments. The work of selecting an attachment moved into the routing service; preserving a transport connection additionally depended on the identity and state used above that mechanism.[^mit]

[Callon's July 1980 BBN report](https://brfid.github.io/posts/multihoming/scans/bbn-4466-pp56-57.jpg) explicitly discussed and cited the MIT proposal, bringing its routing-service alternative into BBN's analysis.[^callon-rosen]

## The disputed work was concrete

At the [January 1981 Internet meeting](https://www.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien175.txt), following MIT's Noel Chiappa on growth and hierarchy, Cerf suggested a persistent host identifier alongside a hierarchical address used as a routing hint. Multihoming remained an explicit, unresolved goal.

[Rosen's June 1981 Internet design](https://www.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien188.txt) pursued logical host names independent of the destination network, translated by gateways; he also distinguished flat names from hierarchical internal routing. Compared with MIT's routing service, this placed translation in gateways, with different requirements for distributing information.[^callon-rosen]

Rosen explicitly considered distributing the translation tables, caching recently used mappings, and either obtaining missing information or forwarding a packet to a gateway that held it. His discussion also identified problems with organizing mappings around a host's home network and with the detours that indirect lookup could produce.[^rosen-distribution] The alternatives already extended beyond a complete table of every host at every gateway; their organization and maintenance remained design work.

In July, Sunshine, Cohen and Postel accepted logical naming's value but [challenged Rosen's architecture](https://www.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien191.txt). They questioned requiring constituent networks to interpret common names, substantial host changes, and unresolved gateway selection. They also objected that his model could force hosts on the same local network to communicate through an intervening gateway.[^debate]

These arguments document resistance to particular designs within a shared research program. They do not divide BBN and DARPA into uniform opposing camps: Cerf's own proposals changed, and the ISI critics were developing alternatives to Rosen's approach. No final DARPA rejection of a general host-identity service has been recovered.[^continued]

The identity being preserved could also extend beyond one machine. Rosen allowed a name to cover interchangeable machines, while Grapevine separated continuing service names from particular servers. Saltzer's later naming-and-binding paper distinguished services, nodes, attachment points, and paths.[^services] These related problems help explain why the discussion repeatedly moved between host attachment, process identity, and service location.

## BBN built hosts with multiple interfaces

Alongside the naming proposals, BBN extended its host software's support for multiple attachments. In November 1981, BBN reported a multihomed UNIX VAX running on ARPANET and an internal BBN network. Its TOPS-20 Multinet software supplied a layer between IP and device drivers for multiple interfaces and protocol sharing.[^host-implementations]

These were practical results, with identifiable limits. The VAX's two attachments both used 1822 interfaces; other access technologies required further modules or drivers. BBN also reported a funding gap delaying distribution of a second beta version.[^host-implementations] That delay concerned particular host software and does not establish a funding decision against the broader addressing proposals. The records leave open whether host-group routing was deployed and when an established TCP connection was first demonstrated to survive attachment failure.

## 1822L implemented the binding within ARPANET

[Malis's 1822L protocol](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc878.html#section-2.2.2) gave a logical name H a list of physical host ports. The subnet selected among them. Its IP mapping let Internet senders retain an address encoding that logical name. The division of responsibility was:

| Layer | Identity or routing decision |
|---|---|
| TCP/IP endpoints | Retain the IP address encoding network N and logical name H |
| Internet gateways | Route toward destination network N |
| Destination ARPANET/PSN | Map H to an available physical host port |

The name could survive a port change while inter-network routing still depended on N. Extending this to attachments on unrelated IP networks would require another level of binding or host-specific routing beyond the destination subnet. The access protocol also allowed 1822L and the older 1822 protocol to coexist and interoperate.[^adoption] This was an optional facility at the ARPANET access boundary, distinct from assigning recovery to Internet applications.

Specified in 1981 and revised in 1983, 1822L explicitly credited Rosen's logical-addressing work. Logical-address assignments appeared in 1984; the 1986 PSN specification described logical addressing as an existing facility; *Official Internet Protocols* included the relevant specifications under recommended IP-over-ARPANET in 1987.[^adoption] These records establish implemented mapping, without establishing the first test of an existing TCP connection surviving attachment failure.

## The general standards left the binding incomplete

The 1982 implementation description *[The DARPA Internet Gateway](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc823.html)* used routing tables indexed by destination network. It did not describe BBN's proposed host-first lookup as the gateway's procedure.[^gateway]

Useful components nevertheless appeared in Internet specifications. RIP supported optional host routes with precedence over network routes. The [1989 host requirements](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1122.html#page-61) permitted weak-host behavior: an address could be accepted through an interface other than the one to which it was associated. Combined with suitable routing and recovery, these capabilities could keep an IP address usable through alternate attachments; they did not automatically discover or maintain those alternatives.[^routing]

IP also supplied source-route options. Clark's 1982 implementation guidance suggested obtaining a source route from a name server and carrying it through the TCP/IP interface. The complete MIT routing service did not become a general protocol requirement.[^source-routing] These capabilities establish technical overlap; the cited records do not establish direct descent of RIP or IP's source-route options from the BBN and MIT proposals.

By 1989, *[Requirements for Internet Hosts — Communication Layers](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1122.html#page-28)* made local multihoming optional and explicitly assigned remote multihoming to applications. Here “remote” meant communicating with a multihomed peer. Its application companion recommended trying alternative addresses returned by name resolution.[^hosts] For TCP, opening a connection to another address changed the connection's identity; the recommendation did not guarantee survival of an existing conversation. A continuing host-to-attachments binding remained something particular networks or additional routing arrangements had to supply.

## Host identity remained under discussion as the Internet grew

Compatibility with an operating service became an explicit concern in discussions of further change. Merit's retrospective account describes the 1987 NSFNET solicitation as requiring reliable production service and TCP/IP, with industry cost sharing expected to supplement federal funding.[^nsfnet] These were commitments to the users and technology of an expanding network.

NSFNET's routing choices show how those commitments affected a particular design. Hans-Werner Braun's February 1989 report explains that the backbone used the Exterior Gateway Protocol, EGP, to exchange routing information with regional and peer networks. Its widespread implementation and support for administrative separation were reasons for the choice; routing information was filtered according to policy. The same report anticipated replacing EGP with a new protocol for routing between independently administered networks.[^nsfnet-routing]

The October 1989 proposal for inter-network routing requirements likewise treated compatibility as a condition for introducing changes. It judged a conversion coordinated across the whole Internet impractical, regarded changes to hosts as especially difficult, and called for old and new systems to interoperate during a transition. It still anticipated changes to addressing. This was a requirements proposal, rather than a standard or a decision about a particular multihoming design.[^migration]

Continuing host identity remained an explicit architectural option. The December 1991 architecture-retreat report considered a larger, flat host identifier, with routers obtaining a mapping from that identifier to the host's administrative domain. The report acknowledged the necessary host changes and migration machinery and proposed further investigation, including a prototype gateway for address mapping. This documents continued consideration of the idea; it does not establish that the proposed system was implemented.[^migration]

The history therefore contains both adoption and unresolved scope. Logical addressing became an ARPANET facility, hosts operated with multiple interfaces, and Internet specifications included useful routing and host capabilities. A general service preserving host identity across attachments on different networks remained outside the 1989 requirements. The contemporary debates explain objections to particular designs, while the later records show compatibility concerns alongside continued proposals. The checked sources have not supplied a final rejection or a decision and funding history that explains the broader omission.[^adoption][^host-implementations][^hosts][^continued]
