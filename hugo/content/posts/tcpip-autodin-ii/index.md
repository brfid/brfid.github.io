TCP/IP provides the common rules that allow different computer networks to communicate, forming the basis of the Internet.[^versions] AUTODIN II was a U.S. defense project to give military computer systems a common data service. Its planned network had to distinguish urgent traffic, enforce security classifications, and restrict communication to authorized communities.[^access-equipment]

The records from 1975–82 show those requirements entering TCP specifications and software, while military and research versions diverged. A 1975 committee proposed separating responsibility for the backbone, access equipment, and the overall subscriber service.[^committee] Implementers adapted the controls to newer designs and reported experimental tests; later standards retained some conventions under changed rules. Code and reported experiments do not establish deployment of the planned service.[^reassessment][^implementations][^implementation-evidence][^later]

For the 1982 Defense Data Network (DDN) choice, the Defense Science Board Task Force favored an ARPANET-based system for its operating experience, interconnection, and prospects for continued development.[^decision-reasons][^authorization] It judged secure operation possible for either design; survivability results were models and the cost advantage was estimated.[^security-choice][^comparisons][^ddn-costs]

The records leave the committee recommendations' adoption, the reason for deferring the ordered TCP/IP upgrade, and the DDN decision's final weighting unresolved.[^sequencing][^standardization][^deferral][^comparisons] They do not establish that AUTODIN II caused TCP and IP to become separate protocols, or that protocol divergence caused cancellation.[^split][^comparisons] The [sources-and-scans index](#sources-and-scans) links the cited records and identifies partial copies.

## The intended service and its relationship to ARPANET

The Defense Communications Agency (DCA) managed the project. Applications would share a packet-switching network, with data divided into packets and carried over shared links.[^access-equipment]

In the eventual division of responsibilities, IP (Internet Protocol) carries packets between networks, while TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) gives applications reliable, ordered delivery. Earlier TCP specifications combined functions later divided between the two protocols. A host is a connected computer; a gateway connects networks. Research into connecting networks preceded the AUTODIN II specifications. Cerf's July 1976 access-equipment study applied that research to connecting military hosts with limited changes to their computers.[^versions][^early-internet][^access-equipment]

AUTODIN II also figured in plans for ARPANET, the U.S. defense department's research network. The July 1977 TCP meeting minutes report Cerf proposing that regional ARPANET clusters be joined through gateways to AUTODIN II, replacing ARPANET's long-distance service. DCA's July 1980 newsletter anticipated subscriber transfers beginning in 1983. Its planned ARPANET II would use AUTODIN II, the remaining ARPANET, and commercial networks.[^planned-network]

These plans were conditional, and neither document establishes that the migration occurred. The newsletter directed users who did not need to communicate with ARPANET subscribers toward commercial services. It did not announce that university or contractor users would all have to leave.[^planned-network]

## Dividing technical and contractual responsibilities

The 1975 review linked technical boundaries to contractual responsibility. DCA convened a committee to review the specification on August 26–27, with Cerf and Jon Postel among its members. Robert Kahn prepared the surviving draft report; its recommendations represent the committee's views, rather than his alone.[^committee]

The committee proposed assigning the overall subscriber service to DCA because it included hosts and protocols beyond the backbone contractor's control. The contractor would be responsible for the packet-switching backbone, with performance assessed at a defined boundary. Access equipment, or front ends, would connect subscriber computers to the backbone, be developed under a separate contract, and be checked independently. A reference implementation would provide a basis for checking other implementations.[^committee]

Packet ordering illustrates the connection. Requiring the backbone to restore packets to their original order could make urgent traffic wait for lower-priority traffic. The committee proposed placing that work in hosts, front ends, or other access devices. Its reasons also included failures, independent changes to host protocols, interconnection between networks with different packet sizes and timing assumptions, and end-to-end encryption.[^sequencing]

The March 1979 Segment Interface Protocol (SIP) specification assigned recovery, resequencing, and end-to-end accountability to users or higher protocols, consistent with the committee's recommendation. Without the intervening specifications or a record of DCA's disposition, that agreement does not establish that DCA adopted the committee's reasoning. SIP's executive summary describes extensive redesign, so its 1979 provisions cannot simply be read back into the original system specification.[^sequencing]

Other AUTODIN II work proposed different allocations. Bolt Beranek and Newman's (BBN) 1975 study considered moving services into hosts and using logical subscriber addresses that would remain stable when a subscriber's attachment changed. It also retained substantial error-control, mapping, and state responsibilities in the switching network. These were architectural proposals within AUTODIN II, rather than an advance specification of IP.[^architectural-alternatives]

## What the military controls required

The July 1976 TCP specification by Postel, Larry Garlick, and Raphael Rom identifies itself as the final Type A TCP deliverable for AUTODIN II. It distinguishes three controls with different purposes:[^sri-controls]

- **Security** limited the classifications a user could send or receive.
- **Precedence** set traffic urgency and permitted lower-priority work to be displaced under specified conditions.
- **Transmission control codes (TCCs)** restricted communication to permitted communities, which could overlap.

The controls required checks as well as packet labels. When an application requested attributes for a new connection, TCP would check its authority against an authorization table supplied by the host's operating system. TCP would also check incoming traffic, while the network switch separately checked subscriber eligibility.[^sri-controls]

In the 1976 specification, security could be an absolute level or a ceiling; under a ceiling, successive units of data could carry different permitted classifications. Precedence was directional, and levels could remain unequal when authority prevented equalization. The network leader, the header used to exchange packets with the network, carried four-bit security, four-bit precedence, and an eight-bit TCC.[^sri-controls]

DCA's undated file-transfer statement of work required security and precedence controls during access and transfer. It allowed simultaneous connections with different attributes, but required closing and reopening a connection to change its security or precedence state. Capacity for the highest-priority traffic was a requirement, not demonstrated performance. The statement cites the July 1976 specifications, but its unknown issue date prevents treating it as the cause of their earlier wording.[^dca-requirements]

SRI's June 1976 FTP proposal offered a study and consultation on TCP and terminal protocols, with a proposed DCA review procedure. Later correspondence discusses separate FTP costing and funding, and a September DCA message acknowledges receipt and review. These records do not establish acceptance or an executed contract modification. The finding covers visually read PDF pp. 1–60 of the 71-page correspondence packet; the remaining pages are outside it.[^contract-evidence]

## Influence and divergence in the specifications

The specifications distinguish AUTODIN II's influence from the earlier internetting research and subsequent changes to TCP:

| Document | Relationship to the developing protocols |
| --- | --- |
| Research TCP, December 1974 | Already included internetwork TCP and gateways. |
| SRI AUTODIN II TCP, July 1976 | Specified the military service's security, precedence, and community controls. |
| Research TCP version 2, March 1977, IEN 5 | Described direct descent from the original research TCP, partial overlap with AUTODIN II, and deferral of its security and priority modifications for reassessment. |
| TCP version 4 draft, June 1978, IEN 40 | Explicitly attributed added security and priority fields to known AUTODIN II requirements, while recording differences among research versions. |
| Western Union TCP, document Revision 3, February 1980 | Continued a distinct AUTODIN II branch, with its own format and revised controls. The document's “Revision 3” is not research TCP version 3. |

The early AUTODIN II specification retained fields for future internetting while omitting that treatment from its body. Those fields did not make it an implementation of the later standard. The June 1978 TCP-4 draft also preceded further revisions; it is not another name for the September 1981 TCP standard.[^early-internet][^sri-controls][^reassessment][^western-union][^versions]

Postel's August 1977 proposal for the TCP/IP split argues from layering and an analogy with the ARPANET host/switch interface. It does not name AUTODIN II as its motive. Contemporary speech work and the Sunshine–Cerf correspondence preserved with IEN 11 document other pressures for different communication services; INWG proposals considered alternative divisions of interface and end-to-end functions. A possible relationship remains open, but shared personnel and recurring problems do not establish that AUTODIN II caused the split.[^split]

## Adaptations, software, and reported tests

December 1978 meeting notes say that DTI's TCP could not communicate with TCP-4 implementations because it used version 3 and AUTODIN II security features. They do not isolate the effects of those two differences. On the same page, BBN's Michael Wingfield described a TCP-4 implementation carrying AUTODIN II controls in Internet-header options, ready for testing.[^implementations]

The BBN records document plans, preserved software, and reported experiments:

| Record | What it documents | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wingfield's BBN 3900 development report, August 1978 | A proposed TCP-4 baseline retaining selected AUTODIN II controls, with an interface adapting ARPANET-style switches for experiments. | These are design commitments. The interface arrangement in every later test is unidentified. |
| Wingfield's IEN 98 status report, May 1979 | Reported security, precedence, and TCC features, file transfers over ARPANET and BBN's RCCNET, and gateway tests. | The authorization setting used for each transfer is not identified. |
| Preserved BBN PDP-11 source | Authorization checks, outgoing encoding, and incoming checks. | This is static inspection. Authorization is disabled by default (`Auth` is zero), and the snapshot is not tied to the exact reported build. |
| Joanne Levin's BBN 3664 test report, August 1979 | Reported exercising legal and illegal requests on three Unix hosts, including security, precedence, and TCC checks. | The tests used an experimental network, and the separate raw logs are absent. A TCC input above 255 was truncated, producing a connection where rejection was expected. |

The preserved source has an apparent defect in its preemption loop, and the inspected results do not establish successful preemption. BBN 3664's conflicting version and reference-date labels also prevent treating the records as descriptions of one unchanged build.[^adaptation][^implementation-evidence]

Levin reports a highest total host bandwidth of 22 kilobits per second on the PDP-11/70, for that host, software, and experimental arrangement. This does not measure the planned AUTODIN II network's capacity. A later BBN host-to-switch test plan gives expected results for a proposed experiment, without establishing execution. The retained FTP test-report photographs end before the concluding Results section. These sources do not establish backbone acceptance from the experimental host tests.[^implementation-evidence]

## The upgrade order and the unresolved transition schedule

The department ordered AUTODIN II to adopt standard TCP/IP, but placed that change after the system's initial operational capability:

| Date | Action or document | Qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| December 23, 1978 | Gerald Dinneen ordered standards based on TCP/IP version 4, including military controls, and an upgrade of AUTODIN II's older TCP. | The memorandum called that older protocol “unsuitable for internetwork operation.” Its judgment did not cover every TCP version. |
| April 1979 target | DCA was to return a cost tradeoff analysis of transition timing. Final standards were also expected after review and testing. | The ordered analysis is not held. Anticipated cost and schedule impacts cannot be treated as its findings. |
| September–December 1979 | Meeting notes identify unfinished interfaces for passing security and precedence information. December's DoD Standard TCP announces security, compartmentation, and precedence provisions. | Published editions show continuing development; intermediate working drafts remain possible. |
| February 8, 1980 | Western Union revised its AUTODIN II TCP specification. | It retained a distinct 29-byte header and access arrangements, without demonstrating convergence on standard TCP/IP. |
| April 3, 1980 | The January TCP/IP editions were ratified. TCP/IP implementation in AUTODIN II was placed as soon after initial operational capability as possible. | The order gives no reason for that placement. |

The available documents do not establish whether cost, readiness, or contractual commitments determined the timing. SRI proposed deleting a related information-service transition task, but that proposal change does not establish an executed contract change or explain the TCP/IP schedule.[^standardization][^deferral][^standard-sequence][^western-union]

## Choosing the Defense Data Network's foundation

The 1981–82 comparison considered an expanded AUTODIN II approach and an alternative based on ARPANET and the World Wide Military Command and Control System's Intercomputer Network (WIN). The retained *Defense Data Network Program Plan* is the latter team's submission, abridged and reissued in May 1982 with a post-decision foreword. Its body still anticipates a future DCA comparison. It documents the proposed design, rather than the evaluation board's findings or a completed network.[^ddn-plan]

The ARPANET/WIN alternative offered operating experience alongside unfinished service work. BBN's July 1981 maintenance proposal described concurrent military use, research use, and development. In December, David Clark reported that users at Fort Bragg and COMSAT depended on special adjustments to individual hosts. His planning paper was a discussion draft.[^concurrent-use]

The Defense Science Board (DSB) Task Force favored ARPANET/WIN. Its account sets out several reasons:

| Consideration | The Task Force's reasoning and its limits |
| --- | --- |
| Experience and interconnection | Operating experience, existing networks, accumulated host-interface work, and opportunities for incremental growth favored ARPANET/WIN. |
| Further development | Government ownership and access to an operational experimental network would support continued changes. |
| Security | The security kernel, the core software enforcing isolation, was difficult to verify and recertify after changes. End-to-end encryption could separate much security work from network changes. The Task Force judged secure operation possible for either design. |
| Survivability | Comparative model calculations favored the alternative, with uncertainty about military assumptions and criteria. These were model results, rather than measurements of completed networks. |
| Cost | DCA's normalized estimates favored the alternative. The Task Force questioned their absolute validity because contractor inputs were not firm delivery commitments. |

These reasons do not establish the factors' final ranking. John Stenbit agreed with the selection but warned, within the Task Force report, that its presentation made the decision appear more straightforward than it had been. The Defense Communications Engineering Center's (DCEC) separate account describes incomplete delay criteria through January 1982 and warns of later changes; it cannot settle the criteria used in the final February evaluation. The records do not establish that protocol incompatibility caused cancellation.[^decision-reasons][^security-choice][^comparisons]

A 1983 Naval Postgraduate School thesis paraphrases the missing February 23, 1982 draft *Factor Comparison Narrative Summary Sheet*. It credits AUTODIN II with better large-transfer performance because the alternative's smaller packets created more overhead, and makes the balance of risks conditional on a satisfactory AUTODIN II system being unavailable. The DSB Task Force says its own preference did not depend on AUTODIN II's programmatic deficiencies, and describes DCA's panel as deciding largely without relying on those difficulties. These accounts cannot be combined into a single evaluation verdict.[^selection-tradeoffs][^comparisons]

### What the cost estimates establish

The department publicly described DDN as more survivable and less costly, but the cost figures use different periods and dollar bases:[^ddn-costs]

| Source | Estimate | Basis or limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1983 thesis, relaying the draft comparison sheet | AUTODIN II $588 million; Replica $429 million | Total system estimates in constant fiscal-year 1982 dollars. No scores or weights are given. |
| Air Force's Senate answer | $159 million DDN advantage | Constant fiscal-year 1982 dollars. This matches the thesis's difference; both may derive from the same estimates. |
| Same Air Force answer | $535 million DDN total | Fiscal years 1982–88, escalated dollars. This uses a different basis from the stated advantage. |
| 1990 Inspector General audit, describing the 1982 plan | $421 million | Development and operation, fiscal years 1982–92. |
| DCA/ASD(C3I) response in that audit | $522.9 million | Inflated dollars, fiscal years 1982–88, attributed to a 1982 memorandum. The response says the plan's $421 million figure was soon recognized as wrong. |

The available documents do not reconcile these totals, and the thesis's annual-cost prose differs from its graph. The audit identifies a March 1982 program-office cost comparison, but does not explain the selection. The figures report an expected cost advantage, with substantial uncertainty about the estimates; they do not demonstrate realized savings or cost's final decision weight.[^ddn-costs]

### The decision and the service still to be built

DCA's panel concluded on February 25, 1982, and its director approved the next day. The DSB Task Force reviewed the evaluation concurrently and submitted its concurring recommendation in March. On April 2, Deputy Secretary of Defense Frank Carlucci ordered AUTODIN II terminated and DCA to implement the ARPANET-based DDN plan.[^authorization]

According to the DSB report, AUTODIN II reached partial initial operational capability in July 1981 while testing continued on major components. This establishes that some equipment operated before cancellation, without establishing delivery of the common service envisaged in the earlier plans.[^authorization]

The DDN plan preferred TCP/IP in subscriber hosts, with other access arrangements for older systems. It accommodated SIP subscribers and scheduled further precedence and preemption development. Some subscribers could initially communicate within their community, with gateway interconnection a further possibility. These were provisions and development commitments; deployment requires separate evidence. The integrated DDN backbone was one packet-switching network, which gateways would connect with other networks in a larger internetwork.[^accommodations]

## What persisted in later standards and implementations

The September 1981 standards retained military controls with changed encodings and policies. IP included precedence and a security option covering classification, compartments, handling restrictions, and controlled communities; TCP specified related connection rules. The table compares selected encodings. An octet is eight bits, or one byte.[^standards-survival]

| Source | Selected representation |
| --- | --- |
| SRI AUTODIN II TCP, July 1976 | Four-bit security, four-bit precedence, eight-bit TCC in the network leader. |
| Preserved BBN PDP-11 code | Four-octet option carrying security, precedence, and TCC; conditional authorization. |
| RFC 760, January 1980 | Four-octet security option with two security bits and eight-bit TCC; precedence in the service octet. |
| RFC 791, September 1981 | Three precedence bits in the service octet; eleven-octet security option with a 24-bit TCC and other controls. |

BBN's November 24, 1981 TCP/IP beta distribution has no corresponding authorization path. It copies outgoing IP options without interpreting them and strips incoming options; its design document had deferred option processing. This was a different BBN project from the DCA PDP-11 work. Its contents do not establish a general policy to abandon military controls.[^beta]

Later standards revised the connection rules. In 2000, RFC 2873 instructed TCP to disregard received precedence when establishing and maintaining connections, because packet markings could change under Differentiated Services. RFC 9293 in 2022 treats the old TCP precedence processing as obsolete, while the Differentiated Services specifications retain compatibility conventions for older markings. This does not establish unchanged AUTODIN II behavior.[^later]

## Decisions the available records do not explain

Three groups of missing records could clarify or change important parts of this account:

| Missing record | What recovery could resolve |
| --- | --- |
| Final committee report, DCA disposition, November 1975 system specification and intervening changes | Whether the recommended technical boundaries were adopted, rejected, partly adopted, or developed independently. The separately bound security appendix is a distinct missing item. |
| Cost tradeoff analysis ordered in December 1978, its transmittal, and relevant deliberations | Why the standard-protocol transition was placed after initial operational capability. Advice for an earlier transition could complicate the apparent chain of decisions. |
| February 1982 comparison sheet and DCA evaluation team's final record | Whether the thesis accurately relays the comparison, what criteria were used, and how they were weighed. The March cost comparison, unabridged plan, and cited 1982 cost memorandum could separately clarify the financial bases. |

Failure to retrieve a record does not establish that it never existed. Matching technical provisions at two dates, or further contractor planning, cannot supply an intervening decision. Influence on TCP's military controls and substantial work on adaptation are documented; the missing decisions limit explanations of the protocol split, transition timing, and DDN selection.[^missing-records]

    Postel, IEN 40, [p. 1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien40.pdf#page=1), corroborates the requirement attribution and distinguishes resynchronization procedures across versions. The earlier AUTODIN II mechanism used an exchange within the old sequence space before establishing a new sequence number. Research versions changed that procedure; version 3 removed resynchronization in favor of a quiet period. This history concerns particular synchronization mechanisms. It does not establish an AUTODIN II origin for general reliability, flow control, or every later TCP feature.

    Later departmental evidence also cautions against treating the totals as settled. The DoD Inspector General's *Defense Data Network*, Audit Report 90-097, July 5, 1990, [printed p. 8 / PDF p. 17](https://web.archive.org/web/20190805182326id_/https://media.defense.gov/1990/Jul/05/2001714385/-1/-1/1/90-097.pdf#page=17), identifies the 1982 program plan's $421 million development-and-operation estimate for fiscal years 1982–92. In their response, [printed p. 41 / PDF p. 50](https://web.archive.org/web/20190805182326id_/https://media.defense.gov/1990/Jul/05/2001714385/-1/-1/1/90-097.pdf#page=50), DCA and ASD(C3I) acknowledge that the figure was soon recognized as wrong and cite a 1982 memorandum projecting $522.9 million, inflated, for fiscal years 1982–88. The periods and bases differ, and no held document reconciles these figures with the $429 million relayed by the thesis.
