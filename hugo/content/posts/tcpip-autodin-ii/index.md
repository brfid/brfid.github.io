At a TCP meeting in July 1977, Vint Cerf described a future in which AUTODIN II would replace the cross-country portions of the ARPANET. Regional ARPANETs would remain, connected through gateways to the new defense network. Three years later, the Defense Communications Agency was still telling ARPANET users to expect a transition to AUTODIN II. The Internet protocols were developing within plans for a communications infrastructure whose eventual form remained unsettled.[^planned-network]

AUTODIN II was intended to provide a common data service for Defense Department computer systems, bringing together communications then carried by several specialized networks. It would divide data into packets carried over shared links. Its requirements concerned the urgency and classification of traffic, the communities permitted to exchange it, and the continuity of service under difficult conditions. These requirements helped shape TCP specifications and implementations. They also became part of an effort to reconcile an operational procurement with protocols that researchers continued to revise.

The interaction becomes clearer when we distinguish a protocol's functions from the network expected to use it. In the architecture standardized in 1981, the Internet Protocol, IP, carried packets between host computers, with gateways forwarding them from one network to another. The Transmission Control Protocol, TCP, supplied reliable, ordered communication between applications using that packet service. Earlier versions of TCP combined responsibilities that were subsequently separated. A document called a TCP specification in 1976 therefore needs to be read in its own version history.[^versions]

The architectural questions included the division of responsibility between a network and its users. In August 1975, a DCA-convened committee reviewed the AUTODIN II specification. Its members included Cerf and Jon Postel; Robert Kahn prepared the surviving draft report. The committee wanted the backbone contractor's responsibilities separated from DCA's responsibility for the overall service. That management arrangement had a proposed technical counterpart: distinguish the packet-switching backbone from the equipment connecting subscribers to it.[^committee]

The proposed boundary affected how performance could be assessed. A contractor responsible for moving packets through the backbone could be evaluated on that work. Making the same contractor answer for the behavior of customers' computers and host protocols would tie its performance to systems outside its control. The committee also recommended a separately contracted front end and a reference implementation against which other implementations could be checked. Modularity connected the design of the service with the organization of procurement and certification.

Traffic priority gave the committee a concrete reason to move packet ordering out of the backbone. If an urgent message needed to pass less urgent traffic, the network might have to deliver messages out of sequence. Requiring it to restore their original order could make urgent traffic wait again. The committee recommended that sequencing be handled by the host, a front end, or another access device. It also argued that this arrangement would help contain failures and allow host protocols to change independently of the switching network.[^sequencing]

Interconnection supplied another reason for the same division. Networks could have different maximum packet sizes and timing assumptions. The review argued that host-level sequencing would make fragmentation and end-to-end encryption easier to accommodate across them. A March 1979 AUTODIN II access specification subsequently assigned recovery, resequencing, and end-to-end accountability to users or higher-level protocols. The intervening decision record has not been recovered, but the recommendation and later specification establish a sustained architectural issue within the project.

These discussions drew on experience and ideas circulating through several networking projects. The committee explicitly invoked ARPANET problems, and an AUTODIN II study by the research contractor Bolt Beranek and Newman, BBN, considered alternative arrangements for addressing, error control, and service placement. Some recommendations continued to rely heavily on the switching network. The evidence lets us examine architectural reasoning within AUTODIN II without assuming that the project originated every principle its reviewers advocated.[^architectural-alternatives]

AUTODIN II's influence on particular TCP features is more directly documented. In July 1976, Postel, Larry Garlick, and Raphael Rom delivered a TCP specification to DCA for the project. Its controls distinguished security classification, traffic precedence, and membership in authorized communities. These were related but different decisions: whether a user could communicate at a particular classification, how urgently traffic should be handled, and which subscribers could exchange it.[^sri-controls]

The requirements reached into the interface between applications and TCP. Opening a connection involved requested security and precedence attributes, checked against user authorization. A label in a packet was therefore only one part of the mechanism. The software also needed to decide whether a caller could request that service and whether incoming traffic met the connection's conditions. Cerf's contemporary work on AUTODIN II access equipment described applying existing internetting research to these operational needs.[^access-equipment]

Research TCP soon reconsidered those particular arrangements. Cerf's March 1977 specification described itself as descending directly from the original research TCP, with only partial overlap with the AUTODIN II version. It left the AUTODIN II security and priority modifications out pending further assessment through end-to-end security projects sponsored by ARPA, the Defense Department's research agency. It also described an options field broad enough to accommodate those services alongside other proposed uses. The requirements remained under consideration while their representation and handling changed.[^reassessment]

Postel's June 1978 TCP version 4 draft made the relationship explicit. Its history attributed added security and priority fields to known AUTODIN II requirements, then distinguished that specification from the subsequent research versions. This is direct evidence of influence, as well as a warning against treating successive documents as one unchanged protocol. The separate question of whether AUTODIN II work caused the TCP/IP split remains open: Postel's August 1977 proposal argued from layering and an ARPANET analogy without identifying AUTODIN II as its motive.[^split]

Implementers had to manage the differences among these versions. The December 1978 Internet meeting notes placed two approaches next to each other. DTI's implementation could not communicate with the TCP-4 implementations because it used version 3 and included AUTODIN II security features. BBN's Michael Wingfield, meanwhile, was preparing a TCP-4 implementation that carried AUTODIN II security information in Internet-header options. One combination was reported as incompatible; another was being prepared for interoperability testing.[^implementations]

Wingfield's development report explains the accommodation. It proposed taking TCP-4 as the baseline while retaining selected AUTODIN II controls. An interface layer allowed experiments using ARPANET-style switches in place of AUTODIN II switches. Another arrangement retained the Internet header for gateway experiments. The project was adapting operational requirements to the newer protocols and using experimental infrastructure to develop and test the result.[^adaptation]

The work progressed beyond design descriptions. Wingfield's May 1979 implementation report identified AUTODIN II security, precedence, and transmission-control-code features, and reported file transfers between hosts on ARPANET and BBN's RCCNET. Preserved BBN source contains authorization checks corresponding to those requirements. Joanne Levin's August 1979 test report describes exercising legal and illegal requests on three Unix hosts. Its detailed results include an exception involving a control code truncated at an interface. The distinction matters: there is reported execution evidence, with identifiable limits, rather than a specification alone.[^implementation-evidence]

The coexistence of protocol versions also required an institutional response. On December 23, 1978, Gerald Dinneen directed adoption of Defense Department protocols based on TCP/IP version 4, incorporating security, precedence, and community-of-interest controls. The memorandum connected this standardization to plans for AUTODIN II to serve and interconnect defense systems. It also described the older TCP being developed for AUTODIN II as “unsuitable for internetwork operation” and ordered its upgrade.[^standardization]

The order recognized that bringing the procurement into alignment could have cost and schedule consequences. DCA was to analyze the transition and report by April 1979. The later memorandum ratifying TCP/IP, dated April 3, 1980, placed AUTODIN II's implementation of the standards after its initial operational capability. We can establish the upgrade requirement and the deferred schedule. The analysis needed to explain why that schedule was selected has not been recovered.[^deferral]

The distinction between the AUTODIN II branch and the standard was still substantive in 1980. The AUTODIN II contractor, Western Union, issued a February revision retaining a particular header format, network fields set to zero, and access-interface arrangements different from standard TCP/IP. It also revised earlier security and preemption rules, which governed the displacement of lower-priority communications. The branch continued to develop while remaining technically distinct. Neither its name nor the continued appearance of familiar control fields makes it interchangeable with the standard.[^western-union]

At the same time, research networking was becoming an operational service in its own right. A July 1981 BBN proposal described Internet protocols in use in both military and research environments. Researchers depended on the communications system while continuing to change it. This activity preceded the reassessment that led to AUTODIN II's cancellation. The defense procurement and the research Internet were developing concurrently, with overlapping users, requirements, and engineering work.[^concurrent-use]

In 1981–82, the Defense Department compared an expanded AUTODIN II approach with an alternative based on ARPANET and WWMCCS Intercomputer Network technology. The alternative's program plan preferred TCP/IP in subscriber hosts, with other access arrangements for systems that could not readily adopt it. The resulting choice changed the infrastructure through which defense communications would be consolidated. It also gave the protocols a foundation more closely shared with the research networks.[^ddn-plan]

The reasons recorded for preferring the ARPANET-based alternative were broader than protocol compatibility. The Defense Science Board Task Force emphasized operational experience, prospects for further development, interconnection with existing networks, and the approach to security. It also discussed cost, survivability, and user needs. The report's argument connected future development to an existing experimental and operational community: there was already a network on which improvements could be exercised by varied users.[^decision-reasons]

Security certification made architectural choices consequential for maintenance. The Task Force described difficulties in verifying AUTODIN II's security kernel, the core software entrusted with enforcing its security rules, and revisiting certification when software changed. End-to-end encryption offered a way to separate much of the security work from changes elsewhere in the network. The report considered secure operation possible for either design. Its preference concerned the organization and continuing cost of achieving that security, alongside other advantages of the alternative.[^security-choice]

The comparison nevertheless leaves room for uncertainty about how the final choice was made. Cost estimates favored the ARPANET-based design, but the Task Force questioned the firmness of contractor commitments behind them. Modeling supported the comparison, although an engineering account covering the period through January 1982 described incomplete performance criteria. Task Force member John Stenbit also warned that the published presentation made the choice appear more obvious than it had been. He agreed with the selection while questioning the report's adequacy as an account of its basis.[^comparisons]

DCA's evaluation panel reached its conclusion on February 25, 1982, and the agency's director approved it the following day. The Task Force, which had reviewed the evaluation concurrently, submitted its concurring recommendation in March. On April 2, Deputy Secretary of Defense Frank Carlucci ordered AUTODIN II terminated and directed DCA to proceed with the Defense Data Network described in the ARPANET Replica plan. The recommendation, evaluation, and authorization were distinct stages.[^authorization]

The selected plan retained accommodations for existing subscribers. It provided for AUTODIN II's Segment Interface Protocol and further work on precedence and preemption. Some systems could initially communicate within their own community, with gateway interconnection available as a further development. The common backbone and its participation in a larger internetwork also need to be distinguished: a distributed set of packet switches was not, simply by being distributed, a collection of autonomous IP networks.[^accommodations]

AUTODIN II-associated requirements also remained in the standards. The September 1981 IP specification included ranked precedence and a security option containing classification, compartmentation, handling restrictions, and a code for controlled communities. TCP specified related authorization and connection-processing rules. Their encodings and policies differed from the 1976 specification. What continued was a set of service requirements through several revisions of the machinery intended to support them.[^standards-survival]

Implementation could lag behind those specifications. A preserved BBN TCP/IP beta distribution dated November 24, 1981, had no corresponding authorization path. It passed outgoing IP options through without interpreting them and stripped options on receipt. Its design document had already deferred option processing to a later development stage. This is evidence about one distribution's completeness; it cannot establish a general decision to abandon the requirements.[^beta]

Later developments continued to change the relationship between those requirements and Internet operation. In 2000, RFC 2873 explained that TCP's old precedence checks could conflict with Differentiated Services, which allowed packet markings to change along a path. It instructed TCP to disregard received precedence when establishing and maintaining connections. A separate 1986 dispute over DDN security interfaces shows that adopting a common architecture also left implementation and administrative problems to resolve.[^later]

The history supports a specific connection between institutional commitments and protocol development. AUTODIN II supplied requirements that entered TCP specifications, a procurement whose protocol commitments had to be updated, and a setting in which researchers argued about the placement and control of network functions. Its replacement favored a different technological and organizational basis for the defense service while carrying some of those requirements forward.

This gives us a way to understand the designers' institutional work without attributing intentions their documents do not establish. They made interconnection, independent development, and shared implementations relevant to concrete service and procurement problems. Those choices affected who would build, certify, and maintain different parts of the system. A broader explanation of funding strategy or later commercial development needs evidence directed to those questions. What these episodes show is how architectural generality could serve particular defense requirements: interconnected systems could provide a common service while allowing parts of the technology, and the arrangements for supplying it, to change separately.

[^decision-reasons]: *Report of Defense Science Board Task Force on AUTODIN II*, Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering, December 1982, DTIC AD-A127476, pp. 12–15, 23, 25, 27; [complete DTIC scan, preserved public copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20240705070244id_/https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA127476.pdf). [^security-choice]: DSB Task Force report, pp. 13–14, 18–20, 24–25, 27. [^standards-survival]: RFC 791, pp. 12, 17–18; RFC 793, §§2.9, 3.6, 3.8; Jon Postel, *[DoD Standard Internet Protocol](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc760.html)*, RFC 760, January 1980, p. 17. The versions differ at the field and policy level:

    | Source | Selected representation or rule |
    | --- | --- |
    | SRI AUTODIN II TCP, July 1976 | Four-bit security and precedence, eight-bit TCC in the network leader; security ceilings and directional precedence permitted. |
    | Preserved BBN PDP-11 implementation | A four-octet option containing four-bit security, four-bit precedence, and an eight-bit TCC; conditional authorization checks. |
    | RFC 760, January 1980 | A four-octet security option containing two security bits and an eight-bit TCC; precedence in the service octet. |
    | RFC 791, September 1981 | Three precedence bits in the service octet; an eleven-octet security option with classification, compartments, handling restrictions, and a 24-bit TCC. |

    RFC 793 couples security and precedence to connection handling, while expressly accommodating implementations restricted to unclassified or single-level use. Matching labels, authorization, packet encryption, and a complete security architecture are distinct properties. The standards establish required or permitted mechanisms for their stated contexts; their publication does not establish how widely those mechanisms were used.

[^beta]: *BBN TCP/IP 3.0 Beta-Test Distribution*, tape label dated November 24, 1981; preserved `tape1.tgz` from the CSRG archives, with its own `LABEL`, `history`, `bbnnet`, `include`, and `doc` material. Gurwitz, *[VAX-UNIX Networking Support Project Implementation Description](https://history.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien168.txt)*, IEN 168, January 1981, §§2.1.2, 2.2.2, describes this DARPA-funded VAX implementation and explicitly defers IP option processing while anticipating security and precedence support. [^later]: X. Xiao, A. Hannan, V. Paxson, and E. Crabbe, *[TCP Processing of the IPv4 Precedence Field](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2873.html)*, RFC 2873, June 2000, §§2–4. It describes how changing packet markings can conflict with TCP's earlier precedence checks and instructs TCP to ignore received precedence when establishing and maintaining connections. The AUTODIN II connection is a historical synthesis across the earlier specifications and implementation reports; RFC 2873 does not itself attribute the problem to AUTODIN II. It identifies a possible failure mechanism and a standards response, rather than measuring its prevalence.

    The legacy was already being revised during the 1980s. *[Requirements for Internet Hosts — Communication Layers](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1122.html)*, RFC 1122, October 1989, §3.2.1.6, identifies precedence as a DoD concern while declaring the old link-service mappings in RFC 795 obsolete. *[DoD Security Options for the Internet Protocol](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1108.html)*, RFC 1108, November 1991, §§2.1–2.4, 2.7.3, changes the labeling scheme and acknowledges limited implementation. The named 24-bit TCC of RFC 791 does not simply persist unchanged through every subsequent security option. These are different kinds of continuity and revision.

    The DDN security-interface dispute has a separate unresolved history. BBN 6223, [printed pp. 18–19](scans/bbn6223-pp18-19.jpg), argues for individually addressable hosts behind LAN gateways and criticizes a KI-111 interface restriction attributed to the *DDN Subscriber Guide to Security Services, 1985–1992*, p. 57. Yet the March 6, 1985 BLACKER front-end interface document, reproduced in the December 1985 *DDN Protocol Handbook*, volume 1, AD-A166324, pp. 1-34–1-35, already allows the X.25 destination to identify a next-hop gateway different from the IP destination. The March 21, 1989 edition retains that allowance. A destination-address clause alone does not settle the guide's reported keying or source-address checks; the cited guide remains unrecovered. Neither a remedy introduced after BBN's complaint nor an error by BBN is established. DDN's inherited consolidation and security objectives supply context, but no examined source establishes AUTODIN II ancestry for this particular interface restriction.
